THE OPERATING MODEL

Make trust
legible.

Reference material for teams that want agents to do more work without turning quality into a feeling. Start with the vocabulary — every ATL page uses these six words.

THE VOCABULARY

Six words explain the whole system.

WORD 01

Route

The sequence of skills ATL runs for your request — for example frame → specify → plan. You never pick it; you can always read it in the manifest.

WORD 02

Dose

How much process the work earns: direct (small, isolated, reversible), standard (ordinary feature or bug work), critical (production, auth, external services). Also called the context budget.

WORD 03

Workflow Manifest

The compact plan ATL shows before doing material work: outcome, route, dose, gates, and stop condition.

WORD 04

Evidence packet

The record after work: checks that actually ran, their outputs, what was skipped, and what is still unknown.

WORD 05

Trust verdict

The answer to “is this actually done?” — six plain questions, each backed by evidence, including what remains inferred.

WORD 06

Gate

A checkpoint that physically refuses an action until the required actor and evidence are present. Gates are why “the agent said so” is never proof.

TRUST VERDICT

Six questions before “done”.

01

What changed?

Name files, behavior, configuration, or docs that moved.

02

Why?

Connect the change to the approved intent or observed failure.

03

What could break?

Include compatibility, boundary, rollback, and negative cases.

04

Downstream impact?

Identify consumers, persistence, deployment, and user-visible effects.

05

Objective achieved?

Re-observe the original success condition, not just a static check.

06

What remains inferred?

Separate directly observed contracts from unknown runtime or external behavior.

EVIDENCE LEVELS

One green check is not every proof.

LevelWhat it provesWhat it cannot prove
Locally provenA command or local observation passed.CI, deployment consumer, or production behavior.
CI/build provenConfigured automation produced the artifact.That the consumer reads it as intended.
Consumer provenThe deployment or downstream consumer accepted the shape.Real user behavior through external services.
Runtime provenThe changed path worked in its actual runtime boundary.Unrelated quality or future regressions.
Still unknownThe required observation was unavailable.A complete verdict.
DOSE · CONTEXT BUDGET

Spend attention where risk lives.

Direct

Metadata, routing references, target files, and a proportionate check. Use for isolated, reversible work.

Standard

Selected playbook, specialist skill, relevant contract, and focused tests. Use for ordinary feature or bug work.

Critical

Full evidence, rollback, boundary, and runtime context. Use when deployment, auth, external services, or unresolved risk is involved.

OPTIMIZATION CONTRACT

One prompt. The right route.

LOW INTERRUPTION

Ask only when it matters.

ATL infers intent and makes a narrow assumption unless a human decision or missing observation changes safety.

LOW CEREMONY

Every artifact earns its place.

No extra skill, scan, test, refactor, diagram, or document without a named failure mode it catches.

LOW CONTEXT COST

Stop at sufficient proof.

Read decision-changing context, avoid duplicates, and finish when the acceptance behavior is directly observed.

ONE FRONT DOOR

Same trust bar, different working lens.

Builders get a bounded slice and handoff proof. Developers get contracts, a small diff, and behavioral evidence. Project managers get scope, risks, dependencies, decisions, and gate status. Tech leads get boundaries, alternatives, rollback, runtime evidence, and autonomy implications. Nobody needs to memorize the skill catalog, and no role gets a weaker standard of truth.

✦
WHY THIS REPOSITORY EXISTS

From impressive throughput to inspectable trust.

ATL was shaped by a simple question: if agents can produce thousands of pull requests, what lets an engineer trust the work when they cannot read every line? The Matt Pocock and Lauren Tan conversation about shipping roughly 2,000 PRs made the answer concrete: verification infrastructure, runtime observation, reusable rules, and feedback that moves into the codebase.

ATL is meant to feel like a smart colleague who has your back: approachable in conversation, willing to disagree when the evidence calls for it, and unwilling to call an unobserved result complete.
